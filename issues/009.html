The Headline

Smirnoff Shares Close At Record High After Eight-Ice Session In Kansas City

Short sellers who piled in after the Week 5 collapse are squeezed as volume hits an all-time high; a quarterback is iced for only the second time in league history.

Shares of flavored malt beverages closed at a record on Monday, capping a three-week rally that has erased the losses of the Week 5 collapse and left the sell-side scrambling to revise price targets it had cut only a month ago.

The rout began when the Blood, Sweat & Mini Beers league, which Smirnoff identifies in its annual report as a key customer, went an entire weekend without a starter at zero for the first time in thirty weeks. Institutional money fled the sector on the theory that twelve men had finally learned to set a lineup, and short interest reached levels not seen since the league's founding. Retail investors held.

The first bid came the following Sunday, when four Ices cleared in a single session and Ian, in his capacity as commissioner, put in a voluntary order by leaving a defensive slot empty. Desks read the purchase as a floor. Week 7 traded thin, three Ices and all of them defenses, and one analyst told CNBC the move was a dead-cat bounce, reiterating an underweight rating and advising clients to rotate into light beer.

Then came Sunday. The market gapped up at the noon open and never looked back, with eight starters finishing at zero or worse before the late window, the highest single-session volume in league history and one above the previous record, which had been matched three times between 2023 and 2024. Wide receivers led the advance with four Ices, running backs and kickers contributed three more between them, and one kicker printed a negative, the only position in the session to finish below zero. Demand ran so hot that a quarterback was iced, only the second such print since the league began, in an asset class the market had long treated as essentially risk-free. The shorts, who had pressed their positions after the Week 5 report, were seen on the floor with their heads in their hands.

Fifteen Ices in three sessions has the season running ahead of the 2023 pace, and the full-season record of thirty-eight set that year is now priced in for the Thanksgiving window. One fund manager, reached at the bar, said he had never doubted the league and declined to say how he was positioned in Week 5. Smirnoff again declined to comment, though a spokesman was seen ordering a second round.

The Man Who Got His Money Back By Halloween
Every fantasy league has a rule nobody thinks about until somebody uses it. This fall, one owner in Kansas City did.

Ask anyone in the Blood, Sweat & Mini Beers league what the fifty-dollar rule is and you'll get the same shrug. The weekly high scorer gets paid, has since 2023, and most years the money drifts around the room without anyone keeping count. Ty cashed a few checks in 2024. Ethan cashed a few last year. The buy-in is two hundred dollars, which means four of those checks would pay a man back in full, and in three seasons nobody had managed it before the week before Thanksgiving.

Then came this autumn. Tanner, a league member since the beginning, put up the top score in Week 2, did it again in Week 3, took a breather, and came back for Week 6. On the first Sunday of November, with his fourth high score of the year, he quietly closed the books on his entry fee with six weeks of football still to play. "I didn't set out to do it," he might have said, if anyone had asked him. The league's own forecasting model, which has rated his roster as overachieving for a month, has been wrong about him for four straight weeks and shows no sign of adjusting.

There's a lesson in the ledger, if you want one. Tanner now ranks second in high-score earnings in the league's history, fifty dollars behind Ty, and first among owners who have never won the championship, a group that includes Ian and John T, two men who have spent four years getting paid on Sundays and sent home in December. And at the bottom of the page are Hayden and PJ, who have each collected a single check since 2023. That comes out to less than a dollar a week. The rule, it turns out, was never the problem. It was waiting for somebody to score.
